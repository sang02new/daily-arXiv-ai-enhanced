Abstract: Estimating mutual information in large neural populations is limited by the exponential growth of possible neural response patterns. Here we apply a Monte Carlo framework to make this problem tractable by sampling from a conditionally independent population model fit from single-neuron responses. The estimator accurately recovers analytical ground truth, corrects finite-data bias, and scales to empirical recordings containing 100 to 10,000 neurons from visual cortex, hippocampus, and retina. Abstract: Platelet traction contracts fibrin networks, yet whether contraction promotes or hinders the escape of suspended particles is unclear. We coupled an overdamped Langevin model of platelet--fibrin mechanics to Brownian transport of finite-size tracers through frozen network states. Nine mechanical replicas were evolved at four filopodial traction baselines (0--8.8\,nN), stacked into 5-$μ$m-thick domains of 50 layers and probed at 0--15\,s with 0.5-$μ$m tracers (29,952 trajectories, 100\,s observation). Benchmarked against experimental clot-contraction kinetics, the simulated contraction is post-lag, within one order of magnitude of experimental rates and corresponds to the onset of the contraction phase. Relative to the matched zero-baseline control, every active-baseline state showed greater projected contraction and a larger tracer-accessible volume ($+9.3\%$ to $+27.6\%$; exact sign test $p=0.0039$ per condition). At 15\,s, mean survival at 100\,s fell from 27.3\% to 14.3\% and the restricted mean exit time from 56.5 to 44.0\,s between the lowest and highest baseline, and all nine replicas shortened residence at 8.8\,nN. At lower baselines, responses differed among replicas, and nearly all retained tracers with a classified start were connected to an exit. Contraction therefore enlarges accessible space without uniformly accelerating escape, separating geometric accommodation from transport kinetics.

Abstract: A pressure-swirl nozzle converts the feed pressure into a swirling conical sheet that breaks into ligaments and then into drops. The size of the drops governs the deposition and drift of agricultural sprays, the capture of airborne dust and the combustion efficiency of fuel injectors. The standard tool for measuring the drop size is laser diffraction, which returns a spatial, concentration-weighted distribution at the beam station. However, the dependence of the measured drop size on the nozzle-to-beam distance has not been quantified alongside a Weber-number scaling, so prefactors reported at different distances cannot be compared on an equal footing. To this end, we measure the drop-size distribution of two full-cone pressure-swirl nozzles by laser diffraction at a range of distances, pressures and orientations, together with high-speed imaging of the sheet breakup. We find that the volume-median diameter $D_{v50}$ decreases with the measurement distance $L$ as $D_{v50} \sim L^{-0.18}$ beyond the drop-formation region. A joint fit over both nozzles gives $D_{v50}/d_0 \sim \mathrm{We}^{-0.31}\,(L/d_0)^{-0.21}$, where $d_0$ is the orifice diameter and $\mathrm{We}$ the Weber number, which separates the pressure and distance effects. At a fixed distance the two nozzles collapse onto the $\mathrm{We}^{-1/3}$ scaling of Kooij et al. (2018), and the measured distance dependence may account for part of the offset between our near-field prefactor and the far-field value. Thus, a drop size obtained by laser diffraction is meaningful only when the measurement distance is reported with it, and a spray should be characterized at the actual distance between the nozzle and its target.
